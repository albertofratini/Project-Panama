1. What was Project Panama?

Court documents and subsequent reporting revealed a highly industrial approach to acquiring books: buy physical copies, remove their bindings, scan the pages, and retain the resulting digital text for AI training while recycling the physical books.

7M+

Pirated books in the earlier library

A federal judge found Anthropic's downloading and storage of more than seven million pirated books was not fair use.

Millions

Physical books acquired

Anthropic separately bought millions of print books, according to court filings and reporting.

$1.5B

2025 settlement

Anthropic agreed to a $1.5 billion settlement concerning the pirated-book claims, without admitting wrongdoing.

The key distinction: the court did not simply say “AI training on books is illegal.” It treated lawfully acquired books used to create a new training corpus differently from Anthropic's earlier acquisition and storage of pirated copies.
2024

Project Panama begins

Anthropic pursued large-scale acquisition of physical books and explored destructive scanning as a way to build a clean corpus of human-written text.

JUN 2025

Judge draws the legal line

Judge William Alsup ruled that training on lawfully acquired books was fair use, while the pirated central library was not protected by fair use.

SEP 2025

$1.5B settlement announced

The authors' piracy-related claims were settled for $1.5 billion, one of the largest copyright settlements in the history of the sector.

JAN 2026

The documents become public knowledge

Reporting based on thousands of pages of court documents exposed the mechanics and scale of the book-acquisition operation.

SUMMER 2026

The market spreads

Booksellers in the US, UK, Ireland, Europe and elsewhere began reporting strange, high-volume orders for obscure and out-of-print titles.

2. It wasn't only Anthropic.

The controversy sits inside a broader race for high-quality training data. Court filings and reporting have implicated multiple AI companies in acquiring copyrighted books at scale, including through unauthorized or pirated sources.

The old pipeline

01 / FIND

Locate huge digital collections.

02 / COPY

Download or scrape the works.

03 / STORE

Create a central corpus.

04 / TRAIN

Use text to improve the model.

05 / DEFEND

Argue transformation / fair use.

Why books?

Books offer long-form, edited, coherent human language. They are also rich in reasoning, narrative structure, domain knowledge and vocabulary. As the public web becomes increasingly saturated with machine-generated material, pre-AI books become unusually attractive as “clean” human-authored data.

Anthropic

Found to have downloaded millions of pirated books into a central library; later bought physical books for a separate training corpus.

Meta

Court documents in authors' litigation exposed internal discussions around obtaining large collections of books, including pirated material.

Beyond books

The same copyright fight now extends across journalism, music, images, software and other human-created intellectual property.

3. The weirdest part: a new book-supply market.

The story is no longer just “AI companies stole books.” A parallel commercial ecosystem has appeared in which databases, marketplaces, logistics providers and booksellers can profit from supplying enormous quantities of physical books.

1K → 1M

Reported order scale

ISBNdb was reported in 2026 as offering services to help AI labs source bulk printed-book purchases ranging from roughly 1,000 to 1 million books.

100s–1000s

Odd secondhand orders

Booksellers reported batches containing unrelated, obscure and outdated titles—often with little apparent interest in price.

↑ sales

A financial incentive

Some sellers saw dramatic increases in sales, while others worried that scarce books were being removed from circulation permanently.

Important nuance: “publishers and retailers supported AI destruction” is too broad a claim. The evidence is stronger for booksellers, marketplaces and book-data intermediaries facilitating or benefiting from bulk sales. Major publishers have also sued AI companies. Some individual sellers have participated enthusiastically; others have refused or become alarmed.

The commercial loop

01 / DATABASE

ISBN-level metadata identifies books that exist.

02 / MARKETPLACE

Orders are placed through book marketplaces and sellers.

03 / AGGREGATION

Intermediaries collect thousands of titles.

04 / SCANNING

Bindings can be cut and pages digitized at industrial speed.

05 / RECYCLING

The physical copy may be discarded while the text survives as data.

ISBNdb: the striking pitch

In 2026, reporting described ISBNdb as marketing bulk-book acquisition to AI labs and positioning pre-2022 printed books as exceptionally useful training material. The relevant page was later described as a test of market interest and taken down.

Why sellers say yes

For used bookstores, this can mean clearing inventory that is difficult to sell one-by-one, moving “dead stock,” and getting paid without negotiating. One WSJ-reported dealer said revenue had roughly doubled amid the new demand.

4. The legal paradox.

The most consequential part of Project Panama may be that the physical destruction itself can become part of the legal argument rather than merely evidence of wrongdoing.

Lawfully acquired print books

Judge Alsup held that Anthropic's use of books it had lawfully purchased to create a training corpus was fair use.

Pirated digital books

The judge separately found that creating and maintaining a central library from pirated copies was not fair use.

The replacement logic

One reason the physical-book pipeline mattered legally was that the resulting digital copies replaced the purchased print copies rather than creating an additional market copy.

That creates a bizarre incentive: if a company can buy a physical book, digitize it, and destroy the physical copy, the legal theory may be more favorable than simply downloading the same book from an unauthorized source. That does not mean every such acquisition is legal, ethical, or safe for rare cultural material.

What remains unresolved

  • How copyright law should treat AI training across different jurisdictions.
  • Whether training models creates meaningful market substitution for authors and publishers.
  • How licensing markets should work when the demand is millions of works at once.
  • Who should bear responsibility when a supply chain destroys scarce physical copies.
  • Whether “fair use” in the US should translate into comparable treatment in Europe and elsewhere.

5. The cultural problem.

A book can be data and still be more than data.

Its physical form can carry provenance, annotations, editions, typography, illustrations, marginalia and historical context. A destructive scan preserves text while potentially eliminating everything around it.

And there is a scarcity problem.

If an AI buyer takes thousands of copies out of the market, that is mostly harmless for a mass-market paperback. It is very different for a regional history, obscure academic monograph, signed copy, specialist translation or title with only a handful of surviving copies.
